Price alone is a poor filter right after a fire; licensing, insurance-claim experience, and a written scope matter more.

If you're standing in a scorched kitchen or a smoke-damaged bedroom in The Heights or Meyerland right now, you don't need a sales pitch, you need a way to tell a competent fire restoration crew from someone who shows up with a shop-vac and a business card. Houston has a lot of companies that list "fire damage" on their website. Far fewer actually run the full process: securing the structure, removing soot and charred material, treating smoke odor at the molecular level, and rebuilding what's gone, while documenting every step for your insurance carrier.

## What should you look for in a fire damage restoration company?

Look for five things: 24/7 emergency board-up, documented smoke and soot removal (not just surface wiping), in-house or coordinated reconstruction, real experience working insurance claims, and a written itemized scope before work starts. A company missing two or more of these is a risk on a loss this size.

Board-up and tarping come first because an open roof or shattered window invites weather damage, theft, and further structural loss within hours. Ask specifically how fast they can secure the property, not just whether they "offer" it.

Smoke and soot removal is where amateur jobs fail. Soot is acidic and etches into drywall, grout, and metal within days. A company should describe dry sponging, HEPA vacuuming, and thermal fogging or ozone/hydroxyl treatment for odor, not just "deep cleaning." If reconstruction is needed, find out whether they handle framing, drywall, and finish work themselves or sub it out entirely, since that affects both timeline and accountability.

## How does the fire damage restoration process actually work?

A proper fire restoration job runs through four phases: securing the structure, assessing and removing damaged material, treating smoke odor and soot residue, and rebuilding. Each phase should be documented in writing, because that documentation is also your insurance file.

First, the crew boards up broken windows and tarps roof openings to stop weather intrusion. Next comes assessment: what's structurally salvageable, what has to be demolished, and whether firefighting water left behind a secondary water damage problem that needs separate drying equipment. Then soot and smoke treatment, cleaned and documented to the [ANSI/IICRC S700 Standard for Professional Fire and Smoke Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/), the industry benchmark for this type of work. Finally, reconstruction: drywall, flooring, paint, and trim work to bring the space back to pre-loss condition. ## What mistakes do homeowners make when hiring after a fire?

The most common mistake is hiring the first company that answers the phone without asking for a written scope of work. Verbal estimates leave both you and the insurance adjuster guessing, and that gap tends to turn into a dispute later.

Other red flags: a company that asks for a large cash deposit before any paperwork is signed, one that can't name the standards it cleans to, or one that has no track record working directly with insurance carriers on fire claims. Fire losses involve more moving parts than a simple water leak, usually a structural engineer or city permit office if framing was compromised, so ask directly whether the company has handled a claim at this scale before.

It also helps to confirm licensing and insurance status up front. [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/consumer/disasters.html) publishes guidance on what to expect from a disaster claim, including how contractor documentation factors into settlement.
