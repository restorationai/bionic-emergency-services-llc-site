**TL;DR:** In Houston, water mitigation, the extraction and structural drying phase that happens before any rebuild work, typically costs $1,200 to $18,000 or more. A single room of clean water caught early usually runs $1,200 to $6,500. Contaminated Category 3 water that requires removing drywall, flooring, or insulation can push past $9,000. The biggest factors are how much square footage is affected, what category of water it is, how long it sat before extraction started, and how many drying days the structure needs.

If a pipe let go overnight in a Heights bungalow or a washing machine line failed in Meyerland while you were at work, you need a number before anyone talks about new drywall or flooring. Mitigation is the first invoice, the part where a crew pulls standing water, pulls out anything that can't be saved, and sets equipment to dry the structure to a safe moisture level. That bill is separate from rebuild costs, and Houston homeowners calling about a fresh loss usually want to know what that first phase runs before they agree to anything.

## What does water mitigation cost in Houston?

Most Houston water mitigation jobs fall between $1,200 and $12,000, with severe Category 3 losses running higher. The spread depends almost entirely on how much area got wet, what was in the water, and how fast the extraction crew got there.

Here's how that breaks down by scenario:

| Scenario | Typical Mitigation Cost |
|---|---|
| Single room, Category 1 clean water, caught within hours | $1,200 to $2,800 |
| Multiple rooms, Category 1-2 water, standard 3-4 day dry-out | $2,800 to $6,500 |
| Whole-floor loss, Category 2 water, flooring and drywall removed | $6,500 to $12,000 |
| Category 3 water (sewage backup, floodwater), contaminated materials removed | $9,000 to $18,000+ |
| Extended drying beyond 5 days or added equipment count | +$150 to $400 per day, per room |
| After-hours or weekend emergency dispatch | +$250 to $600 added to the initial response |

These are typical industry ranges, not a quote. Every loss is different, and [BIONIC Emergency Services](/services/water-damage-restoration/) provides a written scope of work before any extraction or drying equipment goes in.

## What drives the price up or down?

Six things move a mitigation bill more than anything else: square footage, water category, dwell time, drying days, materials removed, and whether the call came in after hours.

**Affected square footage.** A flooded bathroom costs less to dry than a flooded den, simply because fewer air movers and less monitoring time are needed. Crews typically plan for one air mover per 10 to 16 linear feet of affected wall, so a bigger footprint means more equipment on the invoice.

**Water category.** The [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) classifies water loss into three categories. Category 1 is clean water from a supply line. Category 2, sometimes called gray water, carries some contamination, like an overflowing washing machine. Category 3, also called black water, includes sewage backups and floodwater, and it requires removing porous materials like carpet pad, baseboard, and sometimes drywall rather than just drying them. That removal labor is the single biggest reason Category 3 jobs cost more.

**How long the water sat.** Water that stood for 24 to 48 hours before extraction has already wicked into drywall, cabinetry, and subfloor. That means more demolition and longer drying cycles than water pulled within the first few hours, which is part of why after-hours response matters for the final bill, not just the comfort of a faster callback.

**Drying days and equipment count.** A standard dry-out runs 3 to 5 days with daily moisture checks. Houston's humidity can stretch that timeline, since outdoor air brought in by a poorly sealed structure works against the dehumidifiers. More days and more LGR dehumidifiers or air movers both add line items.

**Materials removed versus dried in place.** Drywall, insulation, and carpet pad touched by Category 2 or 3 water typically get removed rather than dried, since they hold contamination even after the moisture is gone. Hardwood and some engineered flooring can sometimes be dried in place with specialty mats, which costs more in equipment but less in replacement material later.

## Does insurance pay for water mitigation?

Most homeowners policies cover water mitigation when the damage is sudden and accidental, like a burst pipe or a failed appliance line, but they typically exclude gradual leaks and separate flood damage entirely. Houston sits in a region where that flood exclusion matters: standard homeowners policies do not cover rising floodwater, which falls under a separate National Flood Insurance Program policy through [FEMA](https://www.fema.gov).

For a covered loss, mitigation is usually the easiest part of a claim to get approved, since most policies treat preventing further damage as a requirement, not an option. Insurers want documented moisture readings, photos of the affected area, and an itemized scope before and after drying. That documentation is also what separates a clean reimbursement from a denied line item, so ask whoever you hire to track readings daily rather than just at the start and end.

## What happens during water mitigation?

Mitigation runs through five phases: assessment, extraction, removal of unsalvageable material, equipment setup, and daily monitoring until the structure clears a dry standard.

1. **Assessment and moisture mapping.** A technician checks the water category, walks the affected area with a moisture meter, and documents the starting readings on walls, flooring, and subfloor.
2. **Extraction.** Standing water comes out first, usually with a truck-mounted or portable extractor, since every hour water sits increases how deep it wicks into materials.
3. **Removal of unsalvageable materials.** Wet carpet pad, contaminated drywall, and swollen baseboard come out at this stage if the water category calls for it.
4. **Equipment setup.** Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers go in, sized to the square footage and the materials still drying in place.
5. **Daily monitoring.** A technician returns daily to check moisture readings against a dry standard, adjusting equipment placement until the structure reads dry.

A single room usually clears that standard in 3 to 5 days. For more detail on timelines, see [how long water damage restoration actually takes](/blog/how-long-does-water-damage-restoration-take/).

## What's the difference between mitigation and restoration?

Mitigation is the extraction and drying phase; restoration is the rebuild that follows, like new drywall, flooring, and paint. They're priced and often invoiced separately, which is why a mitigation estimate and a full restoration estimate can look like very different numbers for the same loss.
