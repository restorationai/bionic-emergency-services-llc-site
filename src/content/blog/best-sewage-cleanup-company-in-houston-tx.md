That's a sewer line problem, and the water coming back up is contaminated. In a Houston slab home in Meyerland, the Heights, or Montrose, that backup usually shows up at the lowest fixture in the house, a tub, a shower pan, or a toilet, not a flooded basement like you'd see up north. ## What makes sewage cleanup different from a regular water leak?

Sewage backup is Category 3 black water under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's grossly contaminated with bacteria, viruses, and waste, and it's handled nothing like a clean supply-line leak.

A burst pipe is Category 1 water: it starts clean, and a lot of it can be dried in place. Sewage from a backed-up drain, a sewer line clog, or a toilet overflow beyond the trap is Category 3 from the start. That distinction drives everything that happens next, from the suits the crew wears to what gets torn out of your bathroom. If you want the fuller breakdown of why this isn't a DIY job, we've written about [the health risks of sewage backup](/blog/sewage-backup-health-risks/) in detail.

## Does the company show up with the right protective gear and containment?

A crew trained for Category 3 water arrives in sealed protective suits, respirators, gloves, and boot covers, not a polo shirt and rubber gloves from under the sink.

Before you let anyone start work, look for:

- Full-body protective suits and disposable boot covers
- Respirators or N95-equivalent masks, not just paper dust masks
- Containment barriers separating the affected area from the rest of the home
- A clear plan for bagging and disposing of contaminated debris, not just hauling it to the curb

If a crew walks in without any of that, that's your answer about whether they understand what Category 3 water actually requires.

## Will they remove contaminated materials, or just dry them?

For Category 3 water, porous materials that absorbed contaminated water, drywall, carpet pad, baseboards, often have to come out, not dry in place like a clean-water loss.

This is where corners get cut. Drying a wet subfloor with air movers looks like progress, but if the carpet pad soaked up sewage, drying it doesn't make it sanitary again. A company that understands [the sewage cleanup process](/services/sewage-cleanup/) will tell you upfront what has to be removed and why, not just run fans and call it finished. Ask for this in writing before work starts, along with a written scope, never a verbal promise.

## How do they document the job, and what does your insurance actually cover?

Ask for moisture readings, photos, and a written scope before and after the work, because that documentation is what your insurance adjuster will use to process the claim.

Here's the part a lot of homeowners get wrong: a standard homeowners policy typically does **not** automatically cover sewer or drain backup. That coverage usually requires a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement added to the policy. Check your declarations page for that endorsement before you assume you're covered. The [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/consumer/disasters.html) has consumer guidance on what standard policies do and don't include after a loss. A company that photographs the contamination, logs moisture readings room by room, and hands you a detailed invoice is doing you a favor whether or not the claim ends up covered.
